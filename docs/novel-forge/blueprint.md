# Novel Forge Blueprint

The Blueprint is how a new novel is designed: a guided, top-down flow that settles the novel as a whole before its engine, its engine before its shape, and its shape before the
first volume and chapters. A gate then switches the same project into the **Workspace**, where chapters are written. Read `novel-forge.md` first; this doc holds only the
Blueprint's intent and invariants.

## One novel from the first click

- "New novel" creates a project immediately, and that project opens in the Blueprint. There is no ideas shelf, no story seed and no graduation step: an idea is a project in its
  Blueprint stage, and everything it decides is the project's own data from the first round.
- An imported plan skips the Blueprint and lands in the Workspace. The Blueprint is the recommended path for a new novel, never a precondition for writing one.
- The Blueprint and the Workspace are two views of one project. Both read the same Story Bible pages, entities, facts, plan and decision ledger.

## Phases and altitudes

Seven phases in a fixed order, grouped by altitude:

| Altitude   | Phase      | Decides                                                         |
| ---------- | ---------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The novel  | Idea       | Starting point, taste, concept, premise                         |
| The novel  | Heart      | Theme, ending question, reader promise, working title           |
| The engine | Core       | Protagonist and what stands in their way                        |
| The engine | World      | How the world works: its rules and the cost of power            |
| The shape  | Spine      | The whole novel in movements, the protagonist's change, reveals |
| Up close   | Volume one | The cast, places and factions volume one needs, and its arcs    |
| Up close   | Opening    | Arc one's chapter briefs, the narrative voice, a final check    |

- Nothing detailed is asked before the level above it is settled: a phase is locked until the phase above it is complete, and the author never names a side character before
  knowing what the book is about.
- A completed phase can be reopened (Revisit). A change made there supersedes decisions through the ledger like any other change; nothing is edited in place.
- Each phase is made of steps. A step is one screen that offers options, lets the author steer them, and ends with a lock that turns the chosen option into decisions.

## What each phase decides

### Idea

- **Starting point.** Whatever the author already imagines (a scene, a feeling, "like X but Y", a character, nothing yet) is read back as short chips so a misunderstanding
  shows immediately. The corrected reading is saved as directions every later step reads.
- **Taste.** Either-or pairs shaped by the starting point, answerable with both, neither or "depends" plus a reason, and "a book you gave up on: why?" reasons. Taste
  becomes directions; dislikes become rejected entries.
- **Concepts.** Rounds of four concept cards. The author's own idea is always one of the cards. Each card can be kept, killed with a reason, mixed into another, edited or
  pushed further; the whole round can be steered. Earlier rounds never return to the model.
- **Premise.** One sentence with its load-bearing parts highlighted. Opening a part regenerates alternatives for that part only; the author can write their own. A throwaway
  preview paragraph is available for motivation and is never saved as a decision. Locking the premise completes the Idea phase.

### Heart

- **Theme and ending question.** The theme is the question under the plot; the ending question is what the reader waits the whole novel to learn. The coach says plainly why a
  weak option is weak (for example a mystery that gets solved rather than a question that ends a novel) instead of hiding it.
- **Reader promise.** Up to two drivers (mystery, progression, romance, slice of life, war and adventure, political intrigue, found family), length, tone and editable
  promises to the reader. It is the last whole-novel decision and the one that tailors every later phase (see below).
- **Title workshop.** Titles in several styles, each tied to the decision it came from; star, reject, steer with rules. It checks catalog fit, the author's own library and,
  through web search, similar published titles, and says plainly what it could not check. The result is a working title, asked again after the first chapters and before
  first publish.

### Core

- **Protagonist.** Versions of the same character that differ in the lie they believe, each shown with its crucible: lie, wound, want, need, change, and how they behave in
  chapter one. Only the protagonist and opposition are designed here; supporting cast waits for Volume one.
- **Opposition.** Chosen, never assumed. Five kinds, each with its own form: a person (whose case the author can edit in their own voice), a system, nature or fate, the
  protagonist's own lie, or nothing. "Nothing" is the slice-of-life path: the engine becomes a small-goals ladder, rhythm and gentle stakes.

### World

- Rules first, detail later. The cost rule for power is an identity decision. A power ladder is asked for only when progression is a driver; otherwise the step is about
  society and economy. Places and factions wait for Volume one.

### Spine

- The whole novel as movements, one per volume, with the ending question pinned, the protagonist's change across the movements and a reveal schedule. Movements are sketches;
  only volume one is detailed in the next phase.

### Volume one

- **Cast.** Full cards only for the characters volume one needs; later characters stay one-liners. Every choice shows whether the author or the system made it. Relationship
  ladders are defined here.
- **Places and factions.** Detail where volume one happens, a sketch for the rest. An idea that changes no sentence in the next twenty chapters goes to the backlog.
- **Arcs.** Volume one broken into arcs, each with a purpose and a turn; relationship rungs are placed onto arcs. Later volumes stay spine sketches until the author reaches
  them.

### Opening

- **Arc one briefs.** Only the first arc gets chapter briefs, planned by the existing arc outliner and citing Story Bible pages directly, with POV and purpose. Briefs for later
  arcs are written in the Workspace when those arcs are reached (today through the existing arc outline), never far ahead.
- **Voice.** Three openings of chapter one in different voices. The author picks, pushes and hand-edits a paragraph; the result becomes voice notes and a pacing and tone
  page.
- **Final check.** One check across the whole Blueprint. Arithmetic findings are one click; story findings are offered as A or B, or the author's own fix, or a dismissal that
  requires a reason. Every resolution becomes a decision.

## Reader-promise drivers

The reader promise's drivers are a decision later phases read, so tailoring lives in one place rather than in special cases scattered across steps:

- **Slice of life** makes opposition optional (small goals, rhythm, gentle stakes), turns spine movements into seasons of life and the reveal schedule into milestones, makes
  the power ladder skippable, and makes the checks look for rhythm and small change instead of an antagonist and escalation. A slice-of-life author is never asked for a
  villain.
- **Progression** asks World for a power ladder.
- **Mystery** asks the Spine for a reveal schedule.
- Any driver other than slice of life asks Core what opposes the protagonist; the opposition kind is pre-selected from the promise and freely changeable.

## The decision ledger

The ledger (the author sees it as the Notebook) is the Blueprint's memory and the project's record of why the novel is the way it is.

- Entry kinds: **decision** (the author locked it), **direction** (a steer the author chose to keep), **rejected** (killed with the author's reason), **backlog** (deferred
  until later in the novel) and **system** (a detail the system decided on the author's behalf, including the gate).
- A decision records its statement, why, the alternatives rejected, who decided, and a **writer line**: what the decision means for the chapter writer. It links to the pages,
  entities, facts, volumes, arcs and briefs it produced.
- The ledger is append-only. Changing a decision appends a new entry that supersedes the old one; nothing is edited in place except the marker that retires an entry
  (superseded or withdrawn) and the links to what an entry produced, which grow as later steps build on it. Active means not retired, and an entry is superseded at most
  once, so a topic's history is one chain.
- Nothing is deleted. Withdrawing an entry deactivates it with the author's reason and no successor, so a dropped direction does not turn into a ban and a withdrawn
  rejection lifts it; the topic's history still shows it. The author writes directions, rejected ideas and backlog entries directly and may reword a decision or overrule a
  system detail; every other decision comes from locking a step.
- Links address produced content by the keys change-set operations use (a page's section and slug, entity and fact keys, volume and arc keys, brief chapters), so a revert and
  re-apply keeps them, and inserting a chapter renumbers linked briefs with everything else.
- Every Blueprint step's context includes the active ledger, and it is never evicted to fit a budget.
- Chapter packs carry the writer lines of active decisions as their own section, so a decision made in the Blueprint reaches the prose. The section is never evicted and has
  its own cap, which keeps every phase's newest lines first.

## Steering and memory

- Every step has a compact steer box: one input line plus nudge chips, with its message thread shown only once used. A steer applies to the next round only, unless the author
  ticks "Keep as a direction", which writes a direction entry every later step reads.
- The model sees the active ledger, the step's inputs (the earlier decisions and pages it needs), the last four messages of the current step and the current steer. It never
  sees full conversation history or earlier rounds' options.
- Rejected entries reach the model only as a do-not-propose list, never as material to build on.
- Each round is stored so a reload shows the latest options; old rounds are history, not context.
- A step runs one round at a time, and its round starts and locks are serialised. A round that stops without finishing (cancelled, crashed, never
  queued) is settled from its job, so it never blocks the step.

## Identity and detail decisions

- **Identity decisions** define the novel: premise, theme, ending question, reader promise, protagonist, opposition and the cost rule. They offer "In my words" and never
  "Decide for me".
- **Detail decisions** (minor cast names and ages, and similar) allow "Decide for me"; the result is a system entry the author can overrule later.
- Decision cards show a cost-to-change level so the author knows what a later change will touch.

## Generation

- Small whole-novel steps (Idea, Heart) run on the ideation model role, one step at a time.
- The heavy middle phases are produced by a few large, all-seeing passes on the planning model group: Core with World, Spine, Volume one, the opening briefs and the final
  check. Each pass sees everything decided so far, and the author still reviews, steers and locks every step it produced. The Blueprint deliberately does not decompose these
  phases into many narrow prompts: large passes plan as well at a fraction of the cost.
- Every round is a background job; nothing in the Blueprint depends on a long-lived request.
- A large pass feeds several screens that the author reviews and locks one by one. Steering one screen reruns the pass focused on it: that
  screen's part is reworked and every other screen's part is kept exactly as it was. Screens of one pass share one round at a time.

## Materialisation

- Locking a step writes its decisions and materialises what they imply as ordinary project content: Story Bible pages, entities, canon facts, volumes, arcs and chapter
  briefs.
- Materialisation goes through the existing change-set operations, applied immediately as the author's own action, so change history and revert work exactly as for any other
  edit. There is no Blueprint-only storage for novel content.
- Ledger entries link to what they produced, and what they produced is plain Workspace content, editable in the Workspace like anything else.
- One lock writes the step's whole answer. Locking it again replaces what the step's earlier locks wrote on its topics (superseding entry for
  entry, the same option first, and withdrawing the rest), so a revisit never leaves two answers to one question active; a step may narrow which
  topics a lock replaces. Entries the author wrote directly, directions and rejections written while steering, and backlog entries are never
  replaced by a lock.
- The ledger entries and the materialised content of one lock commit together or not at all. Work that can only follow a commit (approving what
  was materialised, queueing a job) runs after it; if it fails, the lock stands and the failure is reported beside it.

## Stage

- A project's stage (Blueprint or Workspace) and each phase's state (done, current, locked, with a lock reason) are computed from data, never stored.
- Phase completion is derived from active ledger topics. A project is in the Workspace once the gate entry exists, or when it has chapter briefs and no Blueprint ledger (an
  import).
- A phase is done when every required step that applies to the novel is done; a step the reader promise rules out (a power ladder without progression) never holds its phase
  back. The first unfinished phase is current and the rest wait behind the nearest unfinished phase above them; a phase finished before a revisit stays done. In the
  Workspace nothing is locked: an unfinished phase is open to reopen.
- An import reports per-phase coverage derived from its content (bible pages, entities, volumes, arcs, briefs); gaps are recommendations, not blockers. Only a missing chapter
  brief blocks generation.

## The gate

- The gate is one page showing the whole design, phase by phase, with the stop test: what happens in chapter one, what the reader is waiting for at the end of arc one, and
  the terminal question. Readiness is checked without a model.
- Opening the Workspace writes the gate entry and switches the menu and home screen. It is a mode switch, not a copy: nothing is moved, duplicated or dropped.
- The Blueprint stays one click away from the Workspace, and any part of it can be reopened after writing has started.
