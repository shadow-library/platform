# The Tidewright's Ledger — baseline chapters 1–20

## 1. The Hum Under the Boards

The storm came in sideways, and the outer stilts of Saltmere groaned like old men climbing stairs. Ivo Marren had the south walk, which meant six lanterns, one rope, and nobody to talk to.

He heard the hum before he saw the flicker. It rose through the boards and into his heels, low as a held note on a cello. Nobody else ever heard it. He had stopped mentioning it at twelve.

The fourth lantern dimmed. Inside the tide-glass the bound water sagged, the way a sleeper's chest sags before a snore turns into a cough. Reed apprentices were meant to ring the bell and wait. Ivo set his palm on the glass instead and breathed with it, slow, slow, until the water remembered its shape and the light steadied.

When he turned, Aldine Voss was standing at the end of the walk with rain running off her hood and her patch. She said nothing. She watched him the whole length of the storm, and only when the wind dropped did she say, "Tomorrow. Before the bell. My workroom."

## 2. The Trials Posted

The notice went up on the refectory door at dawn: Current trials, first spring tide, at the Weir. Twelve Reed apprentices crowded it. Petra Quill read it once and walked away, as if she had written it herself.

"You'll want to practise," she said to Ivo over porridge. "I hear you like to improvise."

"I hear you like an audience," he said.

Warden Osk collected the apprentices at noon and marched them east along the causeway to the Weir. Up close it was less a gate than a cliff of oak and iron with water pouring through its teeth. Osk had kept it for forty years. He moved along its catwalks like a man walking through his own kitchen.

He showed them the lantern cradles cut into the stone, the brass rings worn smooth by generations of hands. "You'll bind here," he said. "The tide won't care who your mother was or how many glasses you've broken. It only cares if you listen."

Petra asked three precise questions about flow rates. Ivo asked none. He was listening to the Weir, and the Weir was humming.

## 3. Salt in the Glass

Aldine's workroom smelled of wet rope and lamp oil. Rows of blank tide-glass stood on the shelves like unlit eyes.

"You forced nothing last night," she said. "Do it again, on purpose."

She gave him a slack tide in a basin. He breathed at it, and the glass cracked. He tried again. It cracked again. On the third crack a sliver nicked his thumb and he swore.

"You're pushing," Aldine said. "A slack tide is a tired animal. You don't shout at a tired animal. You sit beside it until it decides you're furniture."

He sat. He let his breath go long and uneven and then less uneven. The water in the basin began to rise and fall with him, a finger's width, then a hand's. He lifted the fourth glass, and the tide slid into it and stayed.

It was cloudy and small and entirely his. Aldine held it up to her one good eye.

"Patience," she said, "is the only rank that matters. The rest is paperwork." She set the glass on the shelf beside her own. "Again."

## 4. The Current Trial

The spring tide came in fast and brown. Twelve apprentices stood on the Weir's lower catwalk with twelve empty glasses while three examiners in grey coats watched from the gatehouse.

Petra went fourth. Her binding was so clean the examiners forgot to write for a moment. The water slid into her glass like a key into a lock, and a few of the watching journeymen clapped before they remembered they weren't supposed to.

Ivo went ninth. The running tide was nothing like Aldine's basin. It pulled at his breath and tried to set its own pace. He lost it once, and felt the examiners lean forward. Then he stopped trying to hold the whole river and listened for the hum underneath it, and matched that instead.

The glass filled crookedly. It held.

That evening the list went up: Current rank, seven names. Quill was second. Marren was last. Ivo stood in front of it until the lamps were lit, grinning like a fool, and when Aldine passed behind him she knocked once on his shoulder with her knuckles and kept walking.

## 5. Night Rounds

Current ranks walked the outer stilts at night, a mile of plank and rope with the marsh black on both sides. Ivo liked it. The hum was clearer out there, away from the city's noise.

On his third night he saw the lantern.

It was far out past the last marker, where there were no walks and no stilts, only reed and water. It burned blue, a colour no tide-glass made. No boat sat beside it. No one was tending it. It simply hung above the marsh, steady as a star, while the wind moved everything else.

He watched it until the relief came and then went straight to the gatehouse.

Guildmaster Halvard Crane was still at his desk, sleeves rolled, ledgers open. He listened to the whole report without interrupting.

"The marsh plays tricks on tired eyes," he said at last. "Swamp gas. Reflections. Every new Current sees something the first month." He closed the ledger in front of him. "Get some sleep, Marren."

Ivo went. On his way out he looked back once. Crane had not reopened the ledger. He was sitting very still, looking at nothing.

## 6. The Drowned Street

The surge hit the lower quarter an hour before dawn. Water came up Cooper's Lane fast enough to lift a handcart and carry it into a shopfront.

Ivo and Petra reached the corner lantern at the same moment. It was failing, and there was no time to argue about whose it was. They put their hands on the glass together.

It was like rowing with a stranger. Petra breathed short and exact; Ivo breathed long and loose. For a terrible moment the tide slipped between them. Then Petra swore, lengthened her breath to meet his, and the lantern flared white. They held it together until the water turned and slid back down the lane.

Neither of them said thank you. They stood there dripping, embarrassed.

Afterward, while the families were hauling furniture back up the steps, Ivo saw Petra slip down the side canal toward the old net sheds. He followed at a distance. Under the eaves of the last shed she met a figure in a hooded oilcoat. They spoke for less than a minute. Something passed from the hooded hand to hers, and she tucked it inside her coat and walked back the long way.

## 7. Ledger Work

Leaving his post to follow Petra cost Ivo a week in the copying room. The punishment was simple: copy the guild ledgers, year by year, into fresh books, because the old ones were rotting.

It was dull, until it wasn't.

The flood year, nine years back, the year the marsh wall broke and his father vanished, should have been the thickest volume on the shelf. It was the thinnest. Whole signatures had been cut out close to the spine. The entries that remained skipped from the week before the flood to a month after, as if the water had simply not happened.

He took the book to Aldine.

She turned it over in her hands, ran a fingernail down the cut edges, and gave it back.

"Copy what is there," she said. "Stop counting what is not."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Aldine said. "It's advice. It's cheaper." She looked at him for a long moment with her one grey eye. "And it's the only kind I can give you this week."

## 8. What Petra Owes

He found her on the drying roof, pinning wet coats to the lines.

"The hooded man," Ivo said. "Cooper's Lane. The net sheds."

Petra kept pinning. "You followed me."

"I lost a week of my life to ledgers for it. You can at least tell me why."

She finished the row before she answered. Then she told him flatly, as if reading an inventory, that the Brine Syndicate paid for her apprenticeship. Her glass, her fees, her lodging. In return they wanted reports: who was promoted, where the lanterns were weakest, what the Weir's schedule looked like.

"I've told them almost nothing true," she said. "Almost."

Ivo thought about the council, and about Crane closing his ledger without looking at it.

"I won't tell anyone," he said. "For now."

Petra looked at him with something close to hatred. It took him a while to understand that it was worse for her to be owed a kindness than to be caught.

"For now," she repeated, and went back to her coats.

## 9. The Hum Grows

The hum stopped being something Ivo heard only on the stilts. By the first week of summer it woke him in his bunk, a low vibration in the frame of the bed, steady as a pulse.

He mentioned it to Aldine while they were sorting glass.

"Loud?" she asked.

"Louder."

She nodded as if he had told her the price of rope. Then, while she was telling him about growing up on the east marsh, she stopped mid-sentence.

"The boat," she said. "My father's boat. It had a name. Painted on the bow in yellow." She laughed, short. "Gone. Isn't that stupid? Thirty years on that boat."

"It'll come back," Ivo said.

"Probably." She picked up a pencil and a small leather notebook he had never seen before, wrote something, and put it away. Over the next few days he saw her do it again: a line here, a line there, small things, as if she were keeping a ledger of her own.

## 10. Storm Season

The first great storm of the season came in against the tide, the worst combination, and the Weir screamed.

Ivo was on the east catwalk with three other Currents when a gust threw the east lantern's keeper against the rail and broke his arm. The lantern shuddered. Without it, the east sluice would open under the pressure, and the lower quarter would drown again.

Ivo took the glass.

He held it for an hour. The tide hammered and pulled, and he let it, the way Aldine had taught him, and kept his breath under the storm's rhythm where the hum was. His hands went white and then numb. Someone wrapped a blanket around his shoulders and he did not notice. When the relief finally came, two Undertows together, he could not open his fingers and they had to pry them off the glass.

Up in the gatehouse window, Guildmaster Crane had watched the whole hour.

At the council the next morning, Aldine told Ivo later, Crane had said only one name.

## 11. The Undertow Candidates

Crane announced the Undertow candidates in the great hall: two names, which was one more than usual. Ivo Marren. Petra Quill.

The journeymen muttered. Candidates were supposed to have five years at Current. Ivo had barely five months.

"Rank is earned at the Weir," Crane said, "not on a calendar." He looked at Ivo when he said it, and Ivo could not tell whether it was pride or measurement.

That night Petra came to Ivo's door. The hooded man had found her again. He wanted the trial schedule: the day, the hour, which sluice.

"I gave him one," she said. "It's wrong by two days and one sluice. If anything happens at the Weir on the wrong day, they'll know it was me. If nothing happens, they'll think I'm useless."

"Which do you want?"

"I want to pass," Petra said, "without owing anyone anything." She almost smiled. "Including you."

## 12. Beneath the Weir

The Undertow trial was simple to describe. The sluice was closed, the chamber behind it flooded, and a candidate went down with an empty glass, bound a tide in the dark, and came back up.

Petra went first. Ivo counted with the examiners at the rim. At a count of ninety she surfaced, glass in hand, and it was empty. She had run out of breath a heartbeat before the tide settled. She climbed out without a word and sat on the stone with her head down.

Ivo went down into black water.

There was no light and no sound except the hum, louder here than anywhere, so loud it felt like it was inside his teeth. He stopped fighting his lungs and listened. The tide in the chamber turned over slowly, and he turned with it, and when it came round the second time he opened the glass.

He came up at a count of one hundred and four, glass full and glowing faintly green.

Undertow, the examiners wrote. Ivo looked for Petra. She had already gone.

## 13. Petra's Choice

Petra found him three days later at the end of the south walk, the same place the story had started for him, though he did not think of it that way.

"They want a key," she said. "A Weir key. Osk keeps them on his belt. They want one by the new moon."

"And?"

"And I said no." She said it as if she were surprised to hear it. "I said no, and the man laughed and said the Syndicate had paid for eight years of my life and could make the next eight very short."

Ivo waited.

"I'm telling you," Petra said, "because if a key goes missing, I didn't take it. And because you're an Undertow now, and they'll listen to you before me." Her mouth twisted. "That's what failing gets you. You have to borrow someone else's word."

"You'll pass next time."

"Next time is a year," Petra said. "A lot of things drown in a year."

## 14. The Missing Key

On the morning after the new moon, Warden Osk counted the keys on his belt twice and then a third time. One was gone.

By noon the whole guild knew. By evening the guild had decided it was Petra.

The council met in the great hall. Crane presided. The evidence was thin and mostly geography: Petra had been seen near the gatehouse, Petra was a failed candidate, Petra was cold and ambitious and nobody liked her. Nobody knew about the Syndicate, and Ivo did not tell them.

He stood up anyway.

"She was with me," he said, "on the south walk, the whole night. You can check the watch roster." It was half true. She had been with him for two hours of it.

Aldine, from the side of the hall, said, "I checked the roster myself. It's in order."

Crane looked from one to the other. Then he closed the matter for lack of proof, and Petra walked out of the hall without looking at anybody.

## 15. Osk's Warning

Osk found Ivo on the Weir at dusk, oiling the brass rings of the lantern cradles because he could not sleep.

"You copied the flood year," the old warden said. It was not a question.

"What there was of it."

Osk lowered himself onto the catwalk with a grunt and let his legs hang over the water. "The archive under the gatehouse holds the real books. The daily logs, the lantern placements, the sluice readings. It's been sealed nine years on the guildmaster's order. I have a key to everything on this gate except that door."

"Why tell me?"

"Because I'm old," Osk said, "and because some of what's written in the copies is wrong. I was here that night. I remember where the lanterns were. The copies don't."

He stood, joints cracking, and went back along the catwalk. Halfway he turned.

"Aldine has a key to the archive," he called back. "She'll tell you she doesn't."

## 16. The Archive

Aldine told him she didn't, and then gave it to him.

The archive door was old enough that the key was only half the problem. Petra knelt at the lock with a set of picks nobody asked her about and worked for twenty minutes while Aldine held the lamp and Ivo watched the stairs.

Inside, the real books were exactly where Osk had said. Ivo found the flood year and opened it on the table.

The lantern placements for the marsh wall were written in Osk's square hand. The night before the flood, the wall's great lantern stood at its usual cradle. The night of the flood, in a different hand, an order: move the wall lantern to the upper city gate. Signed H. Crane. Below it someone had written the wall's collapse, the number of dead, and the name Tomas Marren, missing.

The copies in the guild library had been forged. The order and the move were gone from them; the collapse was blamed on the storm alone.

Nobody spoke for a long time.

"He moved it," Petra said finally. "He took the light off the wall and put it where the rich people live."

## 17. The Guildmaster's Answer

Crane was waiting for them at the top of the archive stairs. He had not brought guards.

"You opened it," he said. "Good. I've been waiting nine years for someone to."

"You moved the lantern," Ivo said. His voice shook. "My father was on that wall."

"I know who was on that wall." Crane's face did not change, but his hands did; he clasped them behind his back. "The storm was going to break the wall or the upper gate. Not both. I had one lantern to spare and I put it where it would save three thousand people instead of three hundred. Then I made sure nobody could ever prove it, because the three hundred had families, and the families had knives."

"You forged the records," Aldine said.

"I did."

"Then you'll tell the council."

Crane looked at her for a long time. "And if I don't?"

Aldine stepped beside Ivo, close enough that their shoulders touched. "Then we will," she said. "And we won't be kind about it."

## 18. Osk at the Weir

The surge came on a clear night, which was the first wrong thing. The second wrong thing was the sound of the north sluice opening when nobody had ordered it opened.

The stolen key. The Syndicate had timed it for the top of the tide.

Ivo ran the catwalk. Ahead of him, already at the sluice, was Warden Osk, without a glass, with his bare hands on the iron wheel and his breath bound into the water itself, the way only the oldest tidewrights could do.

"Get the lantern," Osk said without turning. "Mine won't last."

Ivo got the north lantern lit and bound, and the sluice began, grudgingly, to close. It took four minutes. When the wheel finally locked, Osk let go of it and sat down on the catwalk.

He did not get up.

Ivo knelt beside him. The old warden's hands were still curled as if around the wheel, and his face was calm, and the water below the Weir ran quiet and ordinary, as if nothing had happened at all.

## 19. Ashes on the Water

The city buried Warden Osk at sea, which in Saltmere meant a burning skiff pushed out through the Weir on the ebb. Half the guild stood on the catwalks. The other half stood on the causeway. Nobody spoke while it burned.

Afterward the council met. Crane did not attend; he had been confined to his rooms since the archive. The council named Ivo acting keeper of the Weir until a warden could be chosen. He was seventeen and an Undertow of five months, and nobody objected, because nobody else had been there.

Petra did something nobody expected. She stood up in the great hall and told the whole council that the Brine Syndicate had paid for her apprenticeship, that they had asked for the Weir key, that she had refused, and that someone had taken it anyway.

"If you want to expel me," she said, "do it now, while I'm standing up."

The council did not expel her. It did not thank her, either. That, Ivo thought, was probably exactly how she wanted it.

## 20. The Deep Hum

As acting keeper Ivo slept in the gatehouse, a room above the water where the hum came up through the floor all night.

On the fourth night he lay awake and counted, and understood. The hum kept time with his breath. When he breathed slower, it slowed. When he held his breath, it paused. Whatever it was, it was listening to him the way he had always listened to it.

He told Aldine in the morning.

She was quiet for a long time, turning her notebook over in her hands without opening it.

"Deepwater tidewrights," she said at last, "are the only ones who go under the marsh itself. Not the Weir, not the sluices. Under. There are things down there no ledger mentions." She put the notebook in her pocket. "Don't go looking yet. You're not ready, and you're needed here."

That afternoon a runner brought the council's decision. Halvard Crane was suspended as guildmaster pending a full inquiry. Ivo read the notice twice, standing at the gatehouse window, and felt nothing he could name.
