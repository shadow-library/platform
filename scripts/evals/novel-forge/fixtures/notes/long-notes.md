Working title: The Cartographer of Unfinished Roads

Core idea. In the Republic of Varenne, a map drawn by a licensed cartographer does not describe a road. It makes one. Draw a road on a sealed survey sheet, file it with the Bureau of Routes, and within a season the ground rearranges itself to match: stones surface, hedges part, a river grows a ford where the ink said it would. The Republic runs on this. Whoever controls the maps controls where trade goes, where armies can march, which villages live and which ones wither because the road bent away from them.

The protagonist is Mirela Dask. She is twenty-nine, a courier-cartographer, which means she carries survey sheets between the provinces and the capital and is licensed to make small corrections on the road. She was once a full cartographer of the Bureau. She lost her full licence six years before the story starts, after a road she drew in the south flooded a valley and drowned a village called Hesk. She has never been sure it was her fault. The Bureau was very sure.

Mirela is dry, observant, stubborn, a little funny when she is tired. She hums when she draws. She is left-handed, which matters because survey ink smears for left-handed cartographers, and she has a habit of drawing backwards, right to left, to avoid it. Other cartographers find this unnerving. She does not drink. She does keep a flask of survey ink on a cord around her neck, which is technically illegal outside a Bureau office.

Point of view: third person limited, always Mirela. I decided this. No other point-of-view characters, not even for one chapter. I want the reader to know only what she knows.

How the magic works, the rules I want to keep:
Only ink from the Bureau's presses works. It is made from river silt from the capital, Aubrenne, and something the Bureau keeps secret.
A road drawn on a sealed sheet takes a season to appear. Small corrections (a bend, a ford, a milestone) take a few days.
Two roads cannot be drawn through the same ground. The older map wins unless the Bureau formally revokes it.
Erasing a road is possible but slow and it leaves scars: ground that remembers being a road, where nothing grows right.
The cost: every cartographer loses a little of their sense of direction with every road they draw. Old cartographers get lost in their own houses. Mirela has already started to lose it, and she hides it by counting her steps.
Maps drawn by someone who has never walked the ground come out wrong. This is why the Bureau employs couriers at all.

Places:
Aubrenne, the capital, built on an island in a slow brown river. The Bureau of Routes sits on the highest hill, a white building with a copper dome gone green.
The Eastern March, hill country on the border with the Ossary Principalities. Poor, proud, badly roaded on purpose.
Hesk, the drowned village in the south. What is left of it is a lake with a church spire sticking out of it.
Oskel, a market town on the river Tane with a stone bridge that is older than the Republic and was never drawn by anyone. It just exists, which should be impossible.
The Salt Road, the great trade road from the coast to Aubrenne. Everyone's money moves along it.

Characters besides Mirela:
Tavish Orr. Mirela's courier partner. Forty, big, kind, a former soldier who drives the survey wagon. He is the person she trusts most. He betrays her at the end of volume one: he has been selling copies of her survey sheets to the Ossary Principalities for three years. He does it for his sister's debts. He is not a villain, I want him to be understandable.
Surveyor-General Idris Vell. Head of the Bureau of Routes. Sixty. Elegant, patient, frightening in a quiet way. He was Mirela's teacher. He is the one who took her licence. He believes that the Republic survives only if nobody outside the Bureau can draw roads, and he will do almost anything to keep it that way.
Captain Lune Sorrel. An officer of the border guard in the Eastern March. Thirty-five. She distrusts the Bureau because its roads let armies through. She becomes Mirela's reluctant ally in volume two.
Ansel Dask. Mirela's younger brother. Twenty-two, a clerk in the Bureau's archive. Bright, anxious, loyal to Idris Vell. He is the reason Mirela cannot simply burn the Bureau down.
Mother Gwenna Dask. Mirela's mother, dead for fifteen years. She was a Bureau cartographer too. Everyone believes she died of fever in the Eastern March.

Factions:
The Bureau of Routes. Owns the presses, licenses cartographers, files every map. Its seal is a compass rose with one point missing.
The Unmapped. A loose movement of villages that refuse roads altogether and pay smugglers to erase roads drawn toward them. The Bureau calls them bandits. Some of them are.
The Ossary Principalities. The neighbouring states across the eastern border. They have no cartographers of their own and would give almost anything for Bureau ink.

What I know about the plot, by volume:

Volume one (the courier year). Mirela and Tavish carry sheets through the Eastern March. Roads start appearing that nobody filed: short spurs, a ford, once a whole switchback up a hill. Someone is drawing roads without the Bureau's seal. Mirela is ordered to find out who. She suspects the Unmapped, who are the obvious answer, and she is wrong. At the end of volume one Tavish's betrayal comes out: he has been selling her sheets across the border, and the Principalities have been using them to learn where the Republic's roads are weak. The unfiled roads are not his doing, though. That mystery carries into volume two.

Volume two (the bridge). Mirela goes to Aubrenne to report and meets Surveyor-General Idris Vell again for the first time in six years. He offers her licence back if she will help him find the rogue cartographer. She says yes, because she needs it. After that meeting, in the middle of volume two, the old bridge at Oskel collapses, the one nobody drew. This is important: the bridge collapse happens after Mirela meets the Surveyor-General, not before, because the whole point is that she suspects him of causing it. Captain Lune Sorrel arrives to investigate the collapse and she and Mirela clash and then work together. Volume two ends with Mirela discovering that the rogue roads are being drawn with ink that did not come from the Bureau's presses.

Volume three (the ink). Mirela and Lune trace the second ink to a hidden press in the Eastern March. Ansel is torn between his sister and Idris Vell. The end of volume three is where Mirela learns that her mother Gwenna did not die of fever. Gwenna drew the Eastern March's secret roads and made the second ink, and Idris Vell had her killed and called it fever. Mirela only learns this at the very end of volume three, not earlier. Everything before that should let the reader suspect Vell without knowing about Gwenna.

After volume three I have not decided. Maybe a fourth volume, maybe not. I do not know how the novel ends and I do not want the tool to decide it for me.

Style and tone: dry, specific, practical, like a travel log written by someone who has been cold for a long time. Short chapters, around three thousand words. No prophecies, no chosen ones. Magic has paperwork. I want dust, mud, road-food, invoices, the smell of wet wool and survey ink.

Things I have decided:
Mirela never gets her full licence back during the story, even though Vell offers it.
There is no romance between Mirela and Lune. They become friends, that is all.
Tavish survives. He is exiled, not killed.
The Hesk flood was not Mirela's fault: someone altered her sheet after she filed it. She finds this out in volume two.
Every chapter title is the name of a road or a place on a road.

Things I have not decided (open questions, please leave them open):
Whether Ansel ends up with his sister or with Vell.
Whether the Unmapped are good or bad on balance.
What the Bureau's secret ingredient in the ink actually is.
How the novel ends.

Random details I like and want to keep:
Survey sheets are sealed with green wax and a thumbprint.
Couriers carry a brass tube called a quill-case for the sheets.
Road-inns in the Eastern March serve a barley soup called march broth that everyone complains about and everyone eats.
Milestones in Varenne are painted white on the side facing the capital and red on the side facing the border.
There is a saying among couriers: "Ink first, feet second, and never the other way round."
Mirela has a scar across her left palm from a quill-case that burst in the cold.
Tavish sings badly and loudly when he drives.
Idris Vell's office has no windows and smells of cedar.
Captain Sorrel rides a grey mare named Patience.

A scene I already see clearly: Mirela kneeling in the mud at a crossroads in the Eastern March, laying a sheet on her pack, drawing a ford backwards, right to left, while Tavish holds a lantern over her and sings. The ford appears three days later exactly where she drew it, and a shepherd's children stand in the river and cannot believe the water only comes to their knees.

A scene I see for the end of volume one: Mirela opens Tavish's quill-case looking for a spare pencil and finds a sheet with her own handwriting on it, copied by someone else, stamped with an Ossary seal. She does not say anything. She drives the wagon for the rest of the day while he sleeps in the back.

A scene I see for volume two: the Oskel bridge going down at dawn. Not a dramatic explosion. The stones simply forget they are a bridge and slide into the river one at a time, quietly, while the market is setting up.

A scene I see for volume three: Mirela and Lune finding the hidden press in a barn, still warm, the ink in the vats a colour neither of them has seen before, something between blue and grey.

Research notes to myself: look up how old survey chains worked, look up medieval road law, the idea of a Roman milestone as a political message.

Other thoughts, messy:
Maybe Lune has a brother in the Unmapped? Not sure. Probably not.
The Principalities could send an envoy in volume two. Or not. Leave it.
Should the ink have a smell? Yes: wet iron.
What does losing your sense of direction feel like, from inside? Mirela's first sign is that she stops being sure which way the river flows in Aubrenne, a city she has known all her life. She checks the current with a stick when nobody is looking.
Idris Vell also loses his sense of direction; he has lost almost all of it. He never leaves his windowless office because he can no longer find his way home. His staff guide him. Nobody outside the Bureau knows this. Mirela figures it out in volume two when she watches a clerk take his arm on the stairs.
The Bureau's missing compass point on the seal: official story is humility. Real story: nobody remembers.
Travel speeds, so the geography stays honest: a survey wagon on a good road does about twenty miles a day, a rider on a fresh horse about forty, a courier on foot in the March in winter maybe eight. Aubrenne to Oskel is four days by wagon. Oskel to the border is another six, more in snow. A sheet filed in Aubrenne reaches the Eastern March in about ten days by the ordinary post and three by Bureau rider.
Food on the road, because I keep wanting to write it: march broth, hard cheese wrapped in nettle leaves, dried plums, the black bread every inn claims is its own recipe. Tavish carries a jar of mustard everywhere and puts it on everything.

A few words about the Republic's politics, so I don't forget:
Varenne is governed by an elected Assembly, but the Assembly cannot build a road without the Bureau, so in practice the Surveyor-General is the second most powerful person in the country. The Assembly hates this and cannot fix it. The First Speaker of the Assembly, a man named Corvin Hale, would love to break the Bureau's monopoly and is quietly funding an inquiry. He appears late in volume two. He is not a good man either; he wants the monopoly for himself.

Money: couriers are paid by the league. Mirela earns a courier's wage, which is about a third of what a full cartographer earns. She sends most of it to the families of Hesk, anonymously. Nobody knows this, not even Tavish. I might reveal it in volume three, or never.

Weather and seasons: volume one runs from late autumn through a hard winter. Volume two is spring. Volume three is high summer and harvest. Roads drawn in winter take longer to appear, which is useful for pacing in volume one.

Things people in this world say:
"May your roads be dry and your maps be old." (A courier blessing.)
"Drawn in Aubrenne" means something fake or imposed from above.
"Walked ground" means something honest, earned.

Some names I want to use for minor characters: a road-inn keeper called Hobb Farrant, a Bureau clerk called Sefa Ilwin who helps Ansel, an Unmapped smuggler called Crane-foot (a nickname), and a child in Oskel called Pim who sees the bridge fall.

Rough chapter sketch for volume one, so I stop losing it:

Chapter one, The Salt Road. Mirela and Tavish leave Aubrenne with a quill-case of sheets for the Eastern March. We see the courier life: tolls, milestones, bad inns, the survey wagon with its broken axle pin that Tavish keeps fixing with wire. Mirela counts her steps across a courtyard she has crossed a hundred times, and we don't know why yet.

Chapter two, The Tane Crossing. They cross the river at Oskel and Mirela notices, again, that the old stone bridge is on no map at all. She has noticed this every time for ten years. This time she writes it down. A child, Pim, sells them apples on the bridge.

Chapter three, March Broth. First night in the Eastern March, at Hobb Farrant's inn. Travellers talk about a ford that appeared on the Lisle stream with no filing. Mirela laughs it off in public and does not sleep.

Chapter four, The Lisle Ford. They find the ford. It is real, clean, well made, and there is no sheet for it in the Bureau's registry. Mirela is ordered by letter to investigate. She draws a small correction on the road nearby to test whether the ground still obeys Bureau ink. It does.

Chapter five, The Switchback. A whole switchback appears up Carrow Hill in a week, far too fast for any known ink. Border guards arrive. Mirela meets Captain Lune Sorrel for the first time here, briefly, and they dislike each other on sight. (Lune's bigger role is volume two; this is only a first glimpse.)

Chapter six, The Unmapped. Mirela goes looking for the Unmapped and finds a village that has erased the road to its own door. The villagers are frightened, not guilty. The smuggler Crane-foot tells her the new roads are not theirs either; the Unmapped want fewer roads, not more.

Chapter seven, Snow on the Salt Road. Winter shuts the passes. Mirela and Tavish are stuck at Farrant's inn for three weeks. This is the warm chapter: cards, bad singing, Tavish talking about his sister. I want the reader to love him here, right before it goes wrong.

Chapter eight, The Quill-Case. The betrayal. Mirela finds the copied sheet with the Ossary seal in Tavish's quill-case. She says nothing and drives. End of volume one.

Maybe a short epilogue where Mirela files her report and leaves Tavish's name out of it. Not sure yet whether that is kindness or cowardice. I like that I am not sure.

Some rules for how the Bureau works day to day, because I keep contradicting myself:
A full cartographer may draw new roads anywhere in the Republic, with a sealed sheet countersigned by a regional surveyor.
A courier-cartographer may only make corrections: bends, fords, milestones, culverts. Never a new road.
Every sheet is filed in triplicate: one copy stays in the province, one goes to the Bureau archive in Aubrenne, one goes to the Assembly. Ansel works in the archive and has read more sheets than anyone alive.
Revoking a road needs the Surveyor-General's personal seal. Only Idris Vell can do it. This matters in volume three.
Cartographers are forbidden to draw roads for their own benefit. Everyone does it a little anyway: a shorter path home, a bridge near a relative's farm.

The Hesk flood, so I get it straight:
Six years before the story, Mirela drew a new road through the southern hills to connect Hesk to the Salt Road. The sheet she filed was correct. Someone added a single culvert to it after filing, in a place where a culvert diverted the river. The valley flooded in the spring. Forty-one people died. The Bureau's inquiry blamed her sheet and never looked at the archive copy. In volume two, Mirela asks Ansel to bring her the archive copy and sees the culvert drawn in a hand that is not hers, right to left like hers but with the wrong pressure. Whoever did it knew how she draws.

Final reminder to myself: the heart of the book is Mirela deciding what a road is for. Roads connect people; roads also let armies in. She has spent her life thinking a map is neutral. By the end she knows every line is a choice about who gets to arrive.
